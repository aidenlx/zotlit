# Extended mind and note-taking

Background reading for the claim that a note system is part of how a researcher thinks, not only a place to store results.

## Cognition outside the head

Clark and Chalmers propose the parity principle: a process in the world that does the work of a mental process is part of the mind [@clarkExtendedMind1998, p. 8]. Their example is Otto, whose notebook does the work of his memory.

Hutchins reaches a similar view from field work. On a ship's bridge, navigation is done by the team together with its charts and instruments [@hutchinsCognitionWild1995]. Risko and Gilbert review the experimental work on when and why people move cognitive work into the world [@riskoCognitiveOffloading2016].

## The slip box as a partner

Luhmann describes his slip box as a partner that can surprise him, because its links bring back notes he had forgotten [@luhmannCommunicatingSlipBoxes1981]. Ahrens turns the method into a workflow for academic writing [@ahrensHowTakeSmartNotes2017]. Forte's system stores notes by project instead, which makes retrieval easier but linking less central [@forteBuildingSecondBrain2022].

## How notes are written matters

Students who typed lecture notes copied more of the lecture word for word and understood less of it [@muellerPenMightier2014]. If the notebook is part of the mind, then what we put into it, and in whose words, matters.
