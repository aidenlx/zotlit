# Citation analysis

How the references in scholarly papers became data, and what the counts can and cannot tell us.

## The citation index

Garfield proposed to index science by its references: for each paper, list the later papers that cite it [@garfieldCitationIndexes1955, p. 108]. A reader can then follow an idea forward in time, across the subject boundaries of a library catalogue.

Price used the same data to describe the literature as a network. Most new papers cite a small and recent research front, and much of the older archive is rarely cited again [@priceNetworksScientificPapers1965, p. 510].

## Measures of relatedness

Two measures relate a pair of papers through their references:

- **Bibliographic coupling.** Two papers are coupled when they cite the same earlier work [@kesslerBibliographicCoupling1963].
- **Co-citation.** Two papers are related when later papers cite them together [@smallCoCitationScientific1973, p. 265].

Coupling is fixed when the papers are published. Co-citation changes as the field reads them, so it can show how the field's view of its own past moves.

## Counting and ranking

Garfield later used citation counts to rank journals, the basis of the Journal Impact Factor [@garfieldCitationAnalysisTool1972]. The *h*-index applies a similar idea to the work of one researcher [@hirschIndexQuantify2005].

Every such count inherits the Matthew effect: credit gathers where credit already is [@mertonMatthewEffect1968]. I treat the counts as a map of attention, not of quality.
