# Literature review: personal knowledge management

A survey of personal knowledge management, from its analogue roots to the digital tools that
brought it into everyday practice.

## The slip-box tradition

Luhmann's slip-box, a physical network of hand-written index cards, sustained a prolific
academic career [@luhmannCommunicatingSlipBoxes1981]. The method relies on linking one card to
another through short identifiers, so every retrieval carries its neighbours with it.

Ahrens translated the slip-box workflow for modern writers and researchers, arguing that
permanent notes are useful only when they are connected to the questions the writer is already
pursuing [@ahrensHowTakeSmartNotes2017].

## Digital successors

Forte's productivity system extended these principles to digital capture and retrieval. Its
emphasis on progressive summarization — distilling notes through multiple passes rather than
filing them once — differs from the slip-box's incremental linking approach
[@forteBuildingSecondBrain2022].

Matuschak and Nielsen asked what would happen if note-taking tools were designed from the
ground up for serious intellectual work, not merely as digital filing cabinets. Their essay
frames personal knowledge management as a design problem rather than a habits problem
[@matuschakTransformativeTools2019].

## Connecting past and present

Bush's 1945 vision of the Memex — a desk-sized machine that lets a researcher build trails of
associated documents — anticipated every system above by decades. The core proposal was that
association, not alphabetical filing, mirrors how people actually think
[@bushAsWeMayThink1945].
