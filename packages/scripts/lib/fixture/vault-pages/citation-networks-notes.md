# Research notes: citation network analysis

Notes on the techniques and history behind mapping scholarly influence through citation links.

## Foundations of citation indexing

Garfield introduced the citation index as an alternative to traditional subject classification,
showing that the references a paper cites form a navigable web of intellectual influence
[@garfieldCitationIndexes1955]. The idea that a citation is a recoverable unit of credit
underpins every bibliometric tool that followed.

Small extended this by measuring co-citation — the frequency with which two documents appear
together in the reference lists of later works. Co-citation clusters reveal invisible colleges
of researchers who may never have read each other directly
[@smallCoCitationScientific1973].

## Why citation counts mislead

Ioannidis demonstrated that a high citation count does not guarantee a finding is true.
Statistical power, selection bias, and flexibility in study design all inflate false-positive
rates in the published literature [@ioannidisWhyMost2005]. A citation graph therefore maps
attention, not necessarily truth.

## Visualising scholarly connections

Rougier and colleagues argued that the visual encoding of data matters as much as the data
itself. Clear figures make the structure of a citation network legible at a glance, while
cluttered ones obscure the clusters they are meant to reveal
[@rougierTenSimpleRules2014].

The semantic web programme proposed machine-readable metadata so that links between documents
carry meaning a computer can follow, not just a human reader
[@bernersLeeSemanticWeb2001].
